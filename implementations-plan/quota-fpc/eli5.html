Implementation plan · ELI5 · quota-fpc

Free daily moves for Dark Forest players

Today, before anyone can play Dark Forest on Aztec, they have to bridge tokens into "fee juice" — real money, real friction, and most curious players bounce off that wall. This plan builds a shared gas tank the app owns: we deploy a small contract, put fee juice in it, and it quietly pays the network fee for each player's transactions — up to a daily per-player allowance. The player just plays. In the corner of the screen they see:

27 free txs left · resets 00:00 UTC

When the allowance runs out, the game falls back gracefully: pay from your own balance if you have one, otherwise here's the existing bridge flow. If the Dark Forest team likes the showcase, they fund the tank properly and their onboarding wall disappears.

The four things worth understanding

1 · Money in is one-way

Fee juice sent to the tank can never be pulled back out — the network simply doesn't allow it. So we fund in small tranches: the first one is money we're comfortable losing entirely, and we only add more once the demo proves itself.

2 · The allowance is per person, per day, enforced by the contract

Each player address gets one "day pass" per day (cryptographically enforced — claiming twice is impossible), each pass holds N free transactions, there's a cap on how many players can claim passes each day, and a ceiling on what any single transaction may cost the tank. Worst-case daily spend is therefore simple arithmetic: players × transactions × ceiling — and the tank's balance is the hard stop above that.

3 · Resets happen by themselves

"Resets at midnight UTC" comes from blockchain time itself. There is no server, no scheduled job, and — after a security review round — no admin key at all. Nothing to run daily, nothing to steal. The trade-off: the allowance numbers are baked in at deployment; changing them means deploying a fresh tank (another reason to fund in small tranches).

4 · One honest limitation

The tank can verify who is spending their allowance, but not what game the transaction plays. Someone could burn their daily allowance on non–Dark-Forest transactions. They gain nothing (fees go to the network, not to them), and the per-person daily cap bounds the waste — but it's a property you should knowingly accept (decision A4 below).

Why this plan is shaped the way it is

Grego's team at Aztec Labs built a sponsorship contract we lean on heavily — its quota bookkeeping is excellent and battle-tested. But it identifies the player in a way that clashes with how Dark Forest's own contracts check "does this player own this planet?": used as-is, every move would be rejected. So we keep their quota machinery and change the plumbing so the game still sees the real player. That one change is why this is a small custom contract rather than a literal copy — and it's exactly what the audit rounds below stress-tested.

Full technical detail lives in the repo at implementations-plan/quota-fpc/plan.md (not part of this page on purpose).

Why tier "mid"

Risk rubric: security sensitivity high (the contract holds unrecoverable funds), irreversibility leaning high (mainnet, no refunds) — normally that argues for the heavier "deep" tier. But the blast radius is small (our own demo build, not the team's production site), the funding is deliberately tiny for the PoC, and a dedicated security hardening pass is already scheduled before any serious money goes in. Two independent audit rounds (below) covered the main risk. Confirmed as mid with you earlier; you can still override to deep before approving.

What the audits changed

Two independent reviewers (a top-tier Claude auditor and Codex from a different model family) attacked the first draft. They found real flaws — the most important: the daily allowance could have been claimed many times by one account (fixed: a per-person cryptographic claim), tomorrow's capacity could be pre-burned today (fixed: a 10-minute grace window instead of a day-long one), a subtle off-by-one gave everyone one extra free transaction (fixed), a failed game move could destroy a player's whole day pass (fixed), laggy syncing could tell active players they were "out of moves" (fixed with an explicit wait-and-retry design), and the original daily-rotation server key was a theft target (fixed by deleting the concept — see thing #3). Every finding is either fixed in the plan or turned into an explicit decision for you below. A final fresh-eyes review of the revised plan then came back conditional approve; its conditions (exact fee math, evidence-based sync states, midnight-edge rules, budget-after-measurement) are folded into the plan and into decisions A1, A9 and A10 below. The plan is ready for your verdict.

The eight phases

Phase 1 · Prove the risky bits — and try the game-only restriction

Two rounds of throwaway experiments on a local test network. Round A answers the standard questions: what happens when a sponsored move fails, how fast the wallet notices its updated allowance. Round B (added at your request) prototypes the "sandwich": the tank itself becomes the transaction's front door, checks that the signed payload only targets Dark Forest contracts, then hands off to the player's own account so the game still sees the real player. If it works, sponsorship becomes un-griefable by outsiders — without touching the game's contracts. Research into the core protocol repo and Nethermind's FPC project already de-risked several pieces (the needed SDK class exists and is proven live); the genuinely novel parts are what the spike tests.

Proves itself by: every question answered with evidence on a live local sandbox — then you pick the shape (sandwich vs. the audited standard design) before any real code is built.

Phase 2 · The tank contract itself

The small Noir contract: day passes, per-day player cap, per-transaction fee ceiling, automatic midnight resets, no admin.

Proves itself by: compiles clean through the repo's real build + lint pipeline.

Phase 3 · The brains package

A small TypeScript package with all the decision logic: which payment source to use, when to wait vs. give up, what each error actually means. Kept out of the game client so it can be properly unit-tested.

Proves itself by: unit tests + lint + typecheck, all green.

Phase 4 · Full dress rehearsal on the sandbox — the gate for real money

Integration tests covering the whole lifecycle, including sponsoring the actual Dark Forest contracts deployed locally. Mainnet is blocked until this passes.

Proves itself by: the full suite green against a live local network, real game moves included.

Phase 5 · Deploy & measurement scripts

One script to deploy the tank, one to measure real transaction costs so the fee ceiling is set from data instead of guesses, and a short funding runbook.

Proves itself by: scripted end-to-end run on the sandbox; measured numbers feed the tests.

Phase 6 · Wiring into the game client

The client already has a dormant "sponsor mode" — we extend it: point it at our tank, teach it the fallback chain (free allowance → own balance → bridge).

Proves itself by: client lint + full build green, plus a best-effort local smoke test.

Phase 7 · What the player sees

The counter badge, a detail panel in settings, and honest copy for the three awkward moments: "still syncing", "out of free moves", "network fees spiking". Exhausted players land in the existing bridge flow, not a dead end.

Proves itself by: lint + build green, screenshots of all states reviewed.

Phase 8 · Mainnet showcase

Deploy, measure against real network costs, fund the first small tranche, dry-run with a canary account, then the real demo: sponsored gameplay from a wallet holding nothing. Ends with a handoff note for the DF team.

Proves itself by: a real mainnet transaction paid by the tank, the counter behaving, the fallback demonstrated. Only starts with your explicit go-ahead.
